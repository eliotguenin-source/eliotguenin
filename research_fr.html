Recherche

Note: This text employs inclusive writing. Neologisms such as *auteurices* (authors) or *toustes* (everyone) are used with the aim of introducing them into everyday language. However, when the majority of the group referred to by a word is female or male, the word is inflected accordingly.

Introduction:

The concept of biopolitics is essential to understanding the stakes of the 21st century, the Capitalocene, and its outputs. Initially defined by Foucault as a form of power that takes into account—and is exercised upon—the bodies and lives of individuals and populations, the term has since been enriched by numerous authors, such as Agamben, Canguilhem, Negri and Hardt, Preciado, and Mbembe. Despite differing perspectives, there is a consensus that biopolitics has become the very essence of Western power: a technology of capitalist domination aimed at controlling bodies and minds.

Furthermore, given that the music industry in the streaming era is largely owned by capitalist entities, contemporary sound productions and the ways in which they are accessed align with its productivist agenda. It is therefore worth asking whether current music and its modes of consumption are inherently biopolitical, and to what extent music streaming transforms sound into a medium of biopolitical control. Moreover, can we envision ways to escape this biopolitics of sound?

Argumentation and Literature:

According to Preciado (2008), our current model—the pharmacopornographic economy—relies on the molecular (pharmaco) and semiotechnical (porno) management of desires. For Negri and Hardt (2000), the productive process of contemporary capitalism takes knowledge, information, culture, and social relations as its raw materials. In this model, the aim is no longer to describe the "natural" aspect of the human, but to reveal the cultural, political, and technical processes through which the body—as an "artifact"—acquires a natural status. Capitalist production involving symbols, language, information, or affects—what Negri and Hardt term "biopolitical production"—is transmitted by individuals through biopolitical labor. This labor consists of sharing, communicating, and exchanging such production, thereby constructing their own "somatheques" by assimilating it in a state of constant self-feedback. Streaming fits squarely within this paradigm; it is essentially a flow of biopolitical productions that appears, at first glance, to be immaterial and infinite—designed for instant, masturbatory gratification and a constant dopamine hit that avoids any friction or frustration. It can be described as a "somatic stimulant" (Preciado 2008)—one that is both productivist and idealist. Streaming functions as a "black box" (Latour 2012), masking its material, extractivist foundations and the control it exerts behind its operational mechanics. Indeed, listening means allowing an infrastructure to penetrate one’s own flesh; sound is not external to the body but is instead a form of ingestion that alters our desires and perceptions—without, however, possessing any "magical" properties (Volcler 2017). Thus, at the massive scale of streaming—managing the medium through which billions of people listen to sound—control becomes biopolitical: it is a matter of governing how this infrastructure penetrates their flesh and mastering the ingestion process that shapes their desires and perceptions. As Makis Solomos (2025) puts it: "Today, sound accompanies human life as never before: it is as if our sound environment has undergone a process of hypertrophy, and the desire to instrumentalize it continues to grow."

This research is significant because the topic is not clearly articulated in the scientific literature. Indeed, the concept of biopolitics is often linked to sound through the lens of sonic weaponry and other means of controlling the bodies of the population via sound. Yet, it is the capitalist control of individual *somatheques*—"the way we experience our bodies and how they articulate with our psyches; a collection of postures, gestures, looks, and images derived from cinema, advertising, or the arts, whether determined by social context or adopted in opposition" (*Parlez-Vous Le Preciado?* November 10, 2020. https://www.centrepompidou.fr/en/pompidou-plus/magazine/article/parlez-vous-le-preciado)—that I wish to explore here; that is, the principle of subjectivation ("To speak of 'subjectivation' is to posit that the subject is not only caught up in a process of becoming, but that this construction defines its very being. 'Subjectivation' refers to a process whereby a stream of consciousness—a floating existence—works upon itself and stabilizes into a specific, recognizable form designated as a 'subject'.") between the fragmented subject, the manufactured subject, the governed subject, and the free subject constructing themselves through 'techniques of the self'" (Sforzini, Arianna. "Subjectivation." In *Catégoriser*, edited by Marlène Bouvet, Florent Chossière, Marine Duc, and Estelle Fisson. Lyon: ENS Éditions, 2024). Through a materialist lens applied to the streaming era, I aim to study the material consequences sound has on individuals, in order to seek evidence of an idealist perception of sound within them.

My work follows in the footsteps of authors such as Juliette Volcler and Makis Solomos, who argue that, as early as the late 1930s, sound served the—clearly fantasized—goal of synchronizing workers' bodies with the productivist logic of capitalism. In our post-Fordist society, this goal is no longer the priority; instead, the aim is to monitor, control, and conform people's lives through sound in order to maximize output and perpetuate the Capitalocene. As Juliette Volcler (2017) states: "Industrial podcasting has shifted listening into the realm of surveillance capitalism [...] When Spotify kindly offers to let you resume listening where you left off, continue on another device, or move on to content you are likely to enjoy, it means that behavioral data about you has already been accumulated, that extraction is ongoing, and that others are deriving financial profit from it. A design not of sound, but of listening." The study will therefore also involve dissecting the internal workings of various applications to reveal their biopolitical nature—specifically, the constant pursuit of controlling the release of certain hormones, such as dopamine, through precise algorithmic mechanisms.

Methodology employed:

In this regard, the aim is to ontologically rethink sound production in the era of the pharmacopornographic Capitalocene, in order to reveal how it belongs to this new—post-industrial and global—economic and social regime, where sex, sexuality, and the body become the central stakes of political and economic activity. Concretely, the premise is that individuals conceive of sound from the perspective of consumers. The goal is thus to gain a material understanding of how a representative segment of contemporary society—comprising individuals from diverse social backgrounds and varying levels of cultural capital (Bourdieu 1979)—perceives its relationship with sound. Through qualitative fieldwork, we will also examine how sound transforms their lives and their way of apprehending the world. We will investigate whether these platforms shape our relationship to listening on a global scale. Furthermore, this research does not overlook the principle of class struggle, given that streaming represents the primary—and virtually universal—means by which the population accesses music. The challenge, then, is to shift from a music defined by social distinction to a practice of technical and theoretical redistribution that is both accessible and sovereign.

Accordingly, a portion of the research will be dedicated to sonic transhumanism. We will analyze headphones and earbuds—key adjuncts to the current streaming system—as a technological extension of the human body through sound. Indeed, human anatomy does not allow for the shutting off of hearing; in short, there are no eyelids for the ears. This makes it a particularly effective and all-encompassing tool for advertising, for instance, as the target individual can hardly escape it. Generative AI tools like Suno can also be viewed as technopolitical prostheses (Preciado 2008) for artistic creation—sources, by definition, of biopolitical productions. As Cléo Collomb and Victor Petit (2012) analyze, drawing on Simondon: "Human and machine have entered into a mutually genetic relationship."

Post-biopolitical dynamics:

It is necessary to devise strategies for somatopolitical struggle ("I speak of somatopolitical struggle to name this new revolt of living bodies: they no longer want their life-force to be exploited by the capitalist-patriarchal necropolitical apparatus." Daumas, Cécile (2019) "Paul B. Preciado: 'Our trans bodies are an act of dissidence against the sex-gender system'." *Libération*.) in the face of this control—following the line of Juliette Volcler’s "radical sound ecology" (2022a)—and to analyze the various theoretical or technical attempts implemented to this end. I will cite examples of artists, practices, or collectives aware of this control, such as ecosystemic performance (Di Scipio). One also thinks of the use of the Sneakernet (the body that physically carries the data) as a way of refusing imposed biopolitical fluidity; it is the body that intentionally glitches to break free from the regime of productivity. Furthermore, it is necessary to envision new ways of listening. Indeed, moving beyond the consumption of sound to *intra-act* with it—to "inhabit the sound," as Vinciane Despret puts it—allows both listener and musician to care for their existence as an entity with something to say. I will also analyze various cultural or artistic programs that seek to break away from this system of capitalist and biopolitical control over sound, such as the Left Front’s 2011 project for art, culture, and information. As Preciado points out, "somatopolitical struggle is the revolt of living bodies that refuse to have their life-force exploited by the capitalist necropolitical apparatus" (2026).
